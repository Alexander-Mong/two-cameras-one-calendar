Fire, or something else? About

Not every hot spot is a fire.

Two NASA cameras in space, Moe (MODIS, the older one) and Vee (VIIRS, the newer one), spot hot places all over Earth every day.

Most are fires. Some are a gas flare, a factory, or a volcano that stays hot all year.

Can you tell which is which?

    The clues that give it away

    • A fire comes in a season and moves on. A few weeks or months of heat, then nothing.
    • A steady source stays on. Hot month after month, often seen at night, and it may glow in the night lights.
    • A volcano stays hot too. The wide view gives it away: a mountain.
    • Even NASA's label is an estimate. It is the best answer key we have, not the final word.

    About this page

    The hot spots come from NASA FIRMS (Fire Information for Resource Management System): every detection from MODIS and VIIRS in 2020. Each place here is a square about 2 km wide, placed by its coordinates only. Counts are satellite detections, not fires.

    How hot the cameras read it comes from the same NASA FIRMS rows: the brightness temperature of each detection's hot channel (MODIS 4 µm, VIIRS I4). It is the temperature of the whole pixel, fire and cooler ground mixed together, not of the flames; a small very hot source reads much cooler than it is, and the channels top out (the newer camera's readings stop at 367 K in these files). On our 50 places the readings themselves barely tell a fire from a steady source; how often the cameras read heat here, especially at night, does much better.

    The answer key is NASA's own label on each detection: vegetation fire, volcano, other steady source on land, or offshore. We show the label most of the place's detections carry. The label is itself an estimate.

    Live pictures from NASA (they stream from NASA GIBS, the Global Imagery Browse Services, while you look): the wide view (NASA HLS, Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2, 30 m, or NASA MODIS Terra or Aqua true color, 250 m), NASA's heat view (VIIRS on Suomi NPP, false color from bands M11, I2 and I1, with VIIRS 375 m thermal anomalies by day and by night from NASA FIRMS), and the night lights (NASA Black Marble, VIIRS, 2016). We acknowledge the use of imagery provided by services from NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS), part of NASA's Earth Science Data and Information System (ESDIS).

    Stored pictures (cut once and kept with this page): the 10 m close view and the 3 km heat picture (short-wave infrared bands 12, 11 and 8A) come from the European Union's Copernicus Sentinel-2 satellites, read from the open archive on Amazon Web Services. Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2020. For each place we picked the three clearest days of 2020; for a fire, days during or just after it burned.
